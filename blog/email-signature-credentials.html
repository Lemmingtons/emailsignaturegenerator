Email Signature With Credentials: Degrees, Licences and Certifications

By Email Signature Generator · Published 9 October 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer: put only the credentials your recipient cares about after your name, separated by commas, in a consistent order: highest academic degree first, then professional licences, then certifications (for example, Jordan Lee, PhD, CPA). Spell out anything in progress on the title line instead (PhD Candidate, Department of Chemistry), and add a licence or registration number on its own line only when your regulator or employer requires it.

Credentials in a signature do one job: they tell the recipient why you are qualified to send this message. Every extra letter costs scanning time, so the right list depends on who reads your email, not on everything you have earned. Example names here are fictional.

Which credentials to include

IncludeUsually leave out
Doctoral and professional degrees that define your role (PhD, MD, JD, DDS)Bachelor's degrees after your name once you are working
Licences the recipient relies on (CPA, RN, PE, registered architect)Expired, lapsed or inactive credentials
Certifications that are a requirement or selling point for the work (PMP, CFP, CISSP)Short courses, badges and online certificates
A master's degree when it is relevant to the reader (MBA for a client pitch, MPH in public health)Honorary titles and memberships that need explanation

If you hold several credentials, two or three after your name is usually the limit before the line wraps on a phone. Pick the ones most relevant to the people you email most.

The order and format to use

  1. Academic degrees first, highest first: Jordan Lee, PhD, MBA.
  2. Then licences, which are granted by a regulator and can be revoked: Jordan Lee, MBA, CPA.
  3. Then certifications from professional bodies: Jordan Lee, CPA, CFP.

Some professional bodies publish their own recommended order (nursing credentialing bodies are one example), and some employers set a house style. Follow those when they exist.

Pick one style for periods and keep it everywhere. PhD and Ph.D. are both correct; style guides differ, and consistency with your website and business card matters more than the choice.

Don't double up titles. Use either the prefix or the post-nominal, not both: Dr. Jordan Lee or Jordan Lee, MD, not Dr. Jordan Lee, MD.

Credentials you are still earning

Letters after your name signal a completed credential, so put anything in progress on the title line in plain words:

Doctoral candidatePhD Candidate, Department of Chemistry, Example University

Avoid informal suffixes such as PhD(c) or ABD in external email; they are not recognised everywhere and read as shorthand. For more student layouts, see the student email signature examples.

Licence and registration numbers

Some professions must show a licence or registration number in business communications, and many clients look for it. Common examples include real estate licence numbers, an AFSL or authorised representative number for Australian financial advisers, an AHPRA registration for Australian health practitioners, an SRA number for solicitors in England and Wales, and an NPI for US clinicians. Rules vary by jurisdiction and employer, so check with your regulator or compliance team before relying on any template.

Put the number on its own short line below your contact details (Licence No. 123456) rather than after your name. Frequently asked questions

What order should credentials go in an email signature?

Academic degrees first (highest first), then licences, then certifications, for example “Jordan Lee, PhD, CPA”. Follow your profession's or employer's convention where one exists.

Should I put my bachelor's degree in my email signature?

Not after your name once you are working. Recent graduates can add a line such as “B.S. in Biology, Example University, 2026” while it helps their job search.

How do I show a PhD in progress?

Use the title line: “PhD Candidate, Department of Chemistry, Example University”. Avoid informal suffixes like PhD(c) or ABD in external email.

Can I use Dr. and PhD together?

No. Use one or the other: “Dr. Jordan Lee” or “Jordan Lee, PhD”.

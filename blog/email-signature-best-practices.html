Email Signature Best Practices 2026

Updated April 2026 · 7 min read

A professional email signature is the digital equivalent of a firm handshake. It builds credibility, drives action, and makes every email you send work harder for you. This guide covers what to include, how to design it, and the technical rules that ensure it renders correctly in every email client.

What to include in a professional email signature

A well-structured email signature contains the essentials without overwhelming the reader. The ideal signature includes:

  • Full name — First and last name, clearly readable
  • Job title — Your role at the company
  • Company name — The business or organisation you represent
  • Phone number — Mobile or direct line (not a general switchboard)
  • Email address — Especially useful when signatures are forwarded
  • Website URL — Your company or personal site

Optional, high-value additions:

  • Profile photo — Builds familiarity in relationship-driven industries
  • LinkedIn profile — Standard for B2B communication
  • Call to action — "Book a meeting", "View my listings", "See our latest work"
  • Company logo — For branded corporate communications

✗ Avoid including

Email signature design best practices

Use a visual hierarchy

Your name should be the most prominent element, followed by your title and company, then contact details. Use font size and weight — not colour — to establish hierarchy. A common pattern: Name at 16–18px bold, title at 14px, contact info at 13px.

Limit your colour palette

Use one primary colour (your brand colour) and stick to it for accents, dividers, and links. Black or dark grey for body text. Avoid more than two colours in a signature.

Use email-safe fonts only

The only fonts guaranteed to render across all email clients are system fonts installed on most devices:

  • Arial / Helvetica
  • Georgia
  • Verdana
  • Trebuchet MS
  • Tahoma
  • Times New Roman
  • Courier New

Web fonts (Montserrat, Lato, Inter) will fall back to system defaults in Outlook, Apple Mail, and other clients — causing inconsistent rendering.

Keep photos professional and small

Use a professional headshot (not a casual selfie). Square or portrait crop, cropped to show face and shoulders. Keep file size under 30KB and use a hosted URL — never embed images as base64 data URIs, as these inflate email size and may trigger spam filters.

Tip: Host your profile photo on your company website, LinkedIn, or a CDN like Cloudinary. This ensures the image loads for all recipients, including those with strict corporate email policies.

HTML technical best practices

Email clients render HTML differently from web browsers. The rules that apply to email signature HTML:

  • Use HTML tables for layout — not divs with flexbox or grid. Tables are the only layout system that renders consistently across Outlook, Gmail, Apple Mail, and Yahoo.
  • Use inline CSS only — Gmail, Yahoo, and some Outlook versions strip <style> blocks and class-based CSS. All styles must be applied as style="" attributes.
  • Do not use background images on divs — Outlook for Windows does not support CSS background-image. Use an <img> tag instead.
  • Always include alt text on images — Many email clients block images by default. Alt text ensures recipients know what the image represents.
  • Set explicit widths and heights on images — Prevents layout shifts when images are blocked or slow to load.
Outlook note: Outlook for Windows uses Microsoft Word's rendering engine. It strips border-radius, ignores CSS margins on images, and does not support advanced CSS. If your signature uses any of these, it will look broken in Outlook. Our generator produces Outlook-compatible code by default.

One call-to-action: the most underused best practice

A call-to-action (CTA) button in your email signature turns every email you send into a subtle marketing touchpoint. Common CTAs:

  • Book a meeting — links to Calendly, Cal.com, or your booking page
  • View my portfolio — links to your work or case studies
  • Download our brochure — links to a PDF or landing page
  • See our latest offer — links to a promotion or product page

Use only one CTA per signature. Multiple CTAs dilute attention and reduce clicks.

Mobile email signature best practices

Over 50% of emails are opened on mobile devices. Ensure your signature is readable on small screens:

  • Keep width at or under 480px for mobile compatibility
  • Use a minimum font size of 13px for body text
  • Make phone numbers clickable with tel: links
  • Make social icon tap targets at least 44×44px
  • Test on iPhone Mail and Gmail mobile apps

What NOT to put in your email signature

  • Inspirational quotes — Often feel generic and unprofessional in business contexts
  • Animated GIFs — Blocked by Outlook and increase email file size significantly
  • Long legal disclaimers — Move to the footer of the email body, not the signature
  • Full address — Include only if required by industry regulations or client expectations
  • Multiple phone numbers — Choose one direct line; too many create decision paralysis
  • Too many social links — Limit to 2–3 most relevant platforms for your role

Frequently Asked Questions

What should you include in a professional email signature?

Full name, job title, company name, phone number, email address, and website URL as a minimum. Optionally: profile photo, LinkedIn URL, and one call-to-action. Keep it under 600px wide and 200px tall.

How big should an email signature be?

Width: 500–600px. Height: 100–180px. Image file size: under 30KB total. Gmail's character limit for signature HTML is 10,000 characters. Most well-formatted signatures are 1,000–3,000 characters.

What fonts should I use in an email signature?

Use email-safe fonts: Arial, Georgia, Verdana, Trebuchet MS, Tahoma, or Times New Roman. Avoid web fonts (Montserrat, Lato, etc.) — they fall back to system defaults in most email clients.

Should I include a photo in my email signature?

Yes, for client-facing or relationship-driven roles. Use a professional headshot, keep it under 30KB, and use a hosted URL (not base64). A photo can increase response rates in B2B communication by making you more recognisable.

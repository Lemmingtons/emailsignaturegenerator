What should a professional email signature include?

Start with your name, role, organisation, and one reliable way to continue the conversation. Add a phone number, website, photo, logo, social link, disclaimer, or call to action only when it serves the recipient. A signature is navigation at the end of an email, not a second advertisement.

Choose with four checks: identity, context, route, restraint

This is the decision framework used on this page. It keeps the signature useful when the examples look equally polished.

1. Identity

Make the sender easy to recognise: name, role, and organisation first.

2. Context

Match the format to the message: compact for reply-heavy work, fuller for introductions and outreach.

3. Route

Give the recipient one sensible next step: reply, call, visit, book, or view a portfolio.

4. Restraint

Remove anything that competes with that next step or buries the contact details.

How to choose the right example

For cold outreach, keep the signature compact and put the CTA in the email body. For relationship emails, a profile photo and calendar link can help. For regulated roles, include the licence or registration details your regulator expects, but keep disclaimers short enough that replies remain readable. Students can use the dedicated student email signature examples to choose details for classes, internships, graduate research, and portfolios.

Essential details

  • Full name and role so recipients know who is writing.
  • Company, practice, school, or organization name where relevant.
  • One reliable phone number and one primary email address.
  • Website, booking link, portfolio link, or location page.

Useful extras

  • Profile photo when trust and recognition matter.
  • Logo when brand consistency matters more than personal identity.
  • LinkedIn for recruiters, consultants, executives, and sales teams.
  • CTA button for demos, bookings, quotes, or calendar slots.

What to avoid

  • Huge banner images that turn every reply into an advertisement.
  • Five or more social links when only one or two matter.
  • Quotes, awards, badges, and disclaimers that bury the contact details.
  • Unhosted local images that recipients cannot load.

What changes between Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail?

The content can stay consistent, but setup screens and rendering details differ. Use the client you actually send from as the final test.

Gmail

Google allows formatted text and images in a desktop Gmail signature and sets a 10,000-character limit; images count toward that limit. Keep image dimensions modest and test the pasted result before setting it as the default.

Outlook

Microsoft supports saved signatures and default choices for new messages and replies. The exact menus vary across classic Outlook, new Outlook, and Outlook on the web, so follow the guide for the version you use.

Apple Mail

Apple Mail supports prepared signatures with formatted text, links, and images. Assign the signature to the correct mail account and check how the receiving client handles the image.

Design rules behind the examples

A good email signature works inside messy email clients. It should still make sense when images are blocked, when a recipient reads in dark mode, when Outlook simplifies CSS, or when a long reply thread compresses the message. The examples above keep names, roles, and direct contact details in real text because text is more reliable than images.

Use brand colour as an accent rather than the whole design. Keep the visual hierarchy simple: name first, role second, company and contact details next, then one optional action. If a recipient can understand who you are and how to respond within two seconds, the signature is doing its job. That is why the examples favour readable text, small images, and links that still work after forwarding.
